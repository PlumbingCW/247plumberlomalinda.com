Plumbing Vent Pipe in the Attic: What Loma Linda, CA Owners Should Know

The vent pipe running through your attic is easy to forget until a ceiling stain or sewer smell shows up. Here is what to look for in Loma Linda heat.

By the Loma Linda 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished 2026-10-23Updated 2026-10-23
Call (909) 505-3225

Where the plumbing vent pipe in your attic goes and why it matters

The plumbing vent pipe in an attic is the upper part of the vent stack, a pipe that rises from your drains through the ceiling and out the roof. It lets air into the drain system and lets sewer gas escape above the house. Depending on the age of the home, it may be ABS, PVC, cast iron or galvanized steel.

Most of the time this pipe does its job without a sound. Trouble shows up as a gurgling drain, a faint sewer smell near a bathroom, or a damp stain on the ceiling around the pipe. Each of those has a different cause, and all of them start with the attic.

The direct answer is simple. If a vent pipe is cracked, disconnected or blocked, the fix is repair or replacement of that pipe section, and the first step is to confirm what is wrong from the attic hatch without stepping on the ceiling.

Why attic vents fail

Attic vents fail for ordinary reasons. Remodelers cut them to fit a duct or a new beam and forget to reconnect them. Roofers shift the flashing where the pipe exits and leave a gap. Plastic joints that were glued poorly start to separate years later. Rodents and birds sometimes nest in an open vent top.

Heat is the local factor. The Redlands weather station, the closest long record near Loma Linda, shows July and August normal highs of 95.4 and 96.2 degrees Fahrenheit. It also averages about 25 days a year at 100 degrees or more, and an attic runs hotter than the air outside. Sun-baked plastic can turn brittle and cracked, so an older ABS vent that has baked for decades is a candidate for inspection.

So the tie-back for you is practical. Look at the vent during a cool morning, and leave attic work in the summer afternoons alone.

What to look for, from the hatch

Open the attic hatch, shine a flashlight, and look before you climb in. These signs point to a vent problem.

  • A pipe that stops short of the roof, or one that ends under insulation with no connection above.
  • A joint with a visible gap, a crack or a stain trail running down the pipe.
  • Dark rings or water marks on the insulation or wood around where the pipe passes through the roof.
  • Daylight showing around the pipe at the roof deck.
  • Droppings, shredded insulation or a nest at the opening.

If you see a wet spot on the ceiling below, do not assume it is the vent. Roof flashing leaks, a leaking supply line or a sweating air duct can all leave similar marks, which is why leak detection is often used to tell them apart.

Notice that two rows call for different trades. A vent pipe through the roof sits between plumbing and roofing, and the person you call should be willing to say which part is theirs.

How to check safely, and where a homeowner should stop

Attic work is its own hazard. Step only on joists or on boards laid across them, because one foot between joists can go through the ceiling. Wear a dust mask, gloves and long sleeves, and take a headlamp so both hands can work.

Keep your hands off any wiring you see. Attic wiring and junction boxes often sit close to plumbing, and electricity near a wet spot is dangerous. If insulation near wires is soaked, stay out and, if you can reach the electrical panel without standing in water, switch off the circuit for that area before anyone goes up there.

Do not climb onto the roof to inspect the vent top. A fall from a roof is a far bigger risk than a leaking pipe. Do not cut, glue or re-route vent pipe yourself, because correct pipe size and slope matter, and a patch that looks tidy can still leave the drain starved for air.

A homeowner can safely photograph the pipe, note its material, check the strap, and tell the plumber exactly where the stain sits. That information shortens the visit.

Loma Linda local note: summer attics and permit questions

The Redlands station normals say a typical July brings about 6.6 days at 100 degrees or higher, and August about 9.4. In practice that means attic spaces in Loma Linda cook for much of the summer. If your vent is old plastic, a plumber may suggest checking the joints in spring or fall, when you can work comfortably, rather than waiting for a failure in August.

On permits, the City of Loma Linda Building and Safety Division states that plumbing work requires its own permit, separate from electrical and mechanical permits. It says a licensed contractor or the property owner must obtain it. The city pages do not describe vent repairs specifically, so permit rules vary, and the division is the place to ask. Its counter phone line is listed on the city website.

If the job means opening a roof, the roofing work may need separate treatment from the plumbing. Ask the plumber who is responsible for resealing the roof penetration, and get that in the same conversation as the price.

Repair options

Repairs fall into three groups. A small fix reseals a gap or replaces a rubber boot at the roof. A section fix cuts out cracked pipe and adds a fresh length with proper fittings. A bigger fix re-runs the vent when a remodel left it cut or oversized.

Because new vent runs are part of plumbing installation, the same code questions about size, slope and distance apply as for any new pipe. Ask the plumber to explain the layout in simple terms before the work starts.

When to call

Call a plumber if you smell sewer gas in the house, if a stain keeps growing, or if you see a broken or disconnected vent from the hatch. Call right away if water is dripping through the ceiling, shut off the main water valve if the drip continues, and keep people out from under it.

Attic vent questions

A vent carries air and gas, not water, so a wet vent usually means a leak elsewhere, rain getting past the roof boot, or condensation on a cold pipe. The pipe may also be a path for water from a nearby supply leak.

A disconnected or cracked vent can release gas into the attic, and it can reach rooms through ceiling gaps. A plumber can confirm with a smoke test or a visual check of every joint.

Only for a quick look from the hatch or on joists. Skip it in hot afternoons, stay away from wiring, and never step between joists. If you are unsure, take photos and let a licensed plumber do the rest.

The nearest long-record weather station in Redlands averages about 25 days a year at 100 degrees or more, and an attic runs hotter than the outdoors. Long heat exposure can make older plastic brittle, so a vent that is decades old deserves an occasional look.

The city says plumbing work needs its own permit, but its pages do not name vent repairs. Permit rules vary, so ask the Loma Linda Building and Safety Division, or ask the plumber how they will handle it.
