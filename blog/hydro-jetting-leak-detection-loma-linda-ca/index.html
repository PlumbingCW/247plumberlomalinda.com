Hydro Jetting and Leak Detection in Loma Linda, CA: What Each Finds

Hydro jetting clears the inside of a drain, and leak detection finds water escaping a pressurized pipe, so they answer two different problems.

By the Loma Linda 24/7 Plumbing teamPublished 2026-10-23Updated 2026-10-23
Call (909) 505-3225

The short answer: one cleans drains, the other finds water loss

Hydro jetting is a drain tool. A hose with a special nozzle sends high-pressure water through a drain or sewer pipe and scrubs grease, scale, and roots off the walls. It treats backups and slow drains.

Leak detection is a supply-side tool. It finds where pressurized water is escaping from a pipe you cannot see, under a slab, in a wall, or underground. It treats a high water bill, a meter that keeps moving, a damp patch, or low pressure.

The two are easy to confuse because both involve water and pipes. The test is simple. If water is going out of the house too slowly, you have a drain problem. If water is going into the ground or walls when it should not be, you have a leak.

Signs that point to a drain problem

Use jetting thinking when the trouble is on the waste side.

  • A sink, tub, or toilet drains slowly or backs up.
  • A drain clears with a snake and clogs again within weeks.
  • Kitchen drains smell greasy and clog repeatedly.
  • Roots are suspected, such as when trees grow near the sewer line.
  • Several fixtures gurgle or slow together.

A camera inspection often comes first, since it shows whether the pipe is coated, rooted, or cracked. The service page on hydro jetting explains the method in more detail.

Signs that point to a leak

Use leak detection thinking when the trouble is on the supply side, with pressurized water.

  • The water meter keeps moving after every fixture is off.
  • The water bill jumped without a change in habits.
  • A warm spot appears on a floor, or a floor feels damp.
  • Water sounds run when nothing is on.
  • Pressure falls with no clear cause.
  • A stain spreads on a ceiling or wall.

A leak does not always show. Water can travel along pipes or soak into soil before it appears anywhere. That is why finding it takes tools, not guesses, and why leak detection uses listening devices, pressure tests, and thermal imaging.

What each method can and cannot do

What problem does it addressBlocked or coated drain pipesWater escaping pressurized pipes
What it findsNothing directly, it cleansLocation of a hidden leak
What it fixesThe blockageNothing directly, it locates
Where it worksDrain and sewer linesSupply lines, slab, wall, yard
Main limitCannot seal a crack or holeCannot clear a clog
Follow-up often neededCamera, then repair if damagedRepair at the located spot

Neither method repairs a pipe. Jetting clears the blockage and leaves any crack as it was. Leak detection points to the spot, and then a plumber repairs or replaces that section.

When one tool can hurt the other problem

Jetting a pipe that is cracked or collapsed can make matters worse. High-pressure water can wash soil away from a broken section or push through a weak joint. That is why a camera look before jetting is wise in an older line. If the camera shows a break, the right answer is repair, not more pressure.

Leak detection does not help a clog. Running a pressure test on a drain line does not tell you where a blockage sits. If someone proposes it for a slow drain, ask what the test is meant to show.

Some homes have both problems. A cracked sewer line can leak wastewater into the soil and also catch roots, so water loss and backups show up together. A plumber who knows both tools can tell which comes first.

Loma Linda local note: city lines and older tracts

Where the problem sits matters. Here the city runs both the water supply and the wastewater collection system. For trouble on the street side, such as water bubbling up at the curb or a stoppage in the public sewer main, the city posts an after-hours number for water, sewer, or street emergencies, (909) 356-3805.

That line is for the public side. A leak inside your walls or a clog in your house line is a private problem.

About 42.5 percent of Loma Linda homes were built before 1980, based on Census estimates for 2020 to 2024. Older tracts mean older supply lines and drain pipes, so both kinds of trouble are plausible. It is worth asking whether your pipes are original before choosing a method.

The city audits water loss in its own pipes each year, and its latest planning document puts that loss at 5 to 14 percent of production for 2021 through 2024. Those gallons leave the city system. A leak at your home is a separate matter on your side of the meter.

How a plumber decides which one to use

A plumber starts with questions. Where is the symptom, and when did it start? Is the meter moving? Does the problem follow water use or a particular fixture? The answers narrow things quickly.

Then comes a look. A drain symptom calls for a camera on the drain. A water-loss symptom calls for a meter check, a pressure test on the supply, and listening devices. Only then does the plumber choose a tool. Beginning with a tool and then looking for a problem wastes time and money.

A neighboring city can differ. The water utility in San Bernardino says in its customer FAQ that the customer owns the underground service line from the property to the meter. In Redlands, the utility customer service line takes meter and street leak reports. Each utility draws the line in its own place, so ask yours.

What homeowners can do and what they should not

A homeowner can read the meter, watch for dampness, run a bucket test on a slow drain, and photograph stains. They can turn off the water at the main if something is actively flooding.

A homeowner should not run a rented jetting hose. The pressure can injure skin and damage pipe. A homeowner should not dig for a leak, because buried lines carry water, gas, and power. Call 811 before any digging. A gas smell means leave the building and call SoCalGas at 1-800-427-2200 or 911 before anyone else.

Cost drivers and when to call

Several things shape the price of either service.

  • Pipe length, depth, and material.
  • How hard it is to reach the cleanout or the leak area.
  • Whether a camera or a pressure test is needed first.
  • Whether concrete, drywall, or landscaping must be opened afterward.
  • After-hours or holiday work.

Policies differ on water damage, with sudden and accidental events treated differently from slow leaks and wear, so ask your insurer and take photos. To get a figure for your property, call (909) 505-3225, and a clear price comes first for whichever service fits. Calls are taken around the clock.
